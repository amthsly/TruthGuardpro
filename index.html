India's 'Fake' Laws

A deep dive into the controversial, archaic, and often draconian laws inherited from the British Raj — and the modern statutes that continue to shape India's criminal justice system.

Colonial Legacy — Laws That Never Left

Over 300 laws from the British era remain in force today. Many are criticised for being outdated, draconian, or misused to suppress dissent. Here are the most contentious.

Indian Penal Code (1860) Sec 124A

Sedition – A colonial tool to silence criticism. Originally meant to protect the British Crown, it now criminalises "disaffection" towards the government.

  • Over 500+ cases registered in the last decade.
  • Often used against journalists, activists, and students.
  • Supreme Court has called for its reinterpretation.

Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act AFSPA

1958 – Grants military sweeping powers in "disturbed areas", including shoot-on-sight and arrest without warrant.

  • Active in J&K, Northeast for decades.
  • Alleged human rights violations documented by Amnesty International.
  • Known as the "draconian law" that shields security forces from prosecution.

Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act UAPA

1967 – Allows detention for up to 180 days without charge. Designed to combat terrorism but increasingly used against political opponents.

  • 95% of UAPA cases in 2020 were non-terror related.
  • Critics call it a "political weapon" to stifle dissent.
  • Over 1,500 people currently held under UAPA.

Prevention of Money Laundering Act PMLA

2002 – Allows investigative agencies to attach and confiscate property before conviction, reversing the presumption of innocence.

  • Enforcement Directorate has used it aggressively.
  • Bail is nearly impossible under PMLA provisions.
  • Critics call it "economic sedition".

Colonial-era Police & Jail Manuals

Outdated prison rules, flogging, and solitary confinement provisions still exist in many state manuals.

  • Starvation diets and overcrowding persist.
  • Reforms have been slow and uneven.
  • Over 75% of undertrials are from marginalised communities.

Modern Manifestations — New Laws, Old Ideas

Recent laws like the Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA), the Farm Laws, and the new criminal codes (replacing IPC, CrPC, and Evidence Act) continue to spark debate about civil liberties and federalism.

Citizenship Amendment Act CAA

2019 – Fast-tracks citizenship for non-Muslim refugees from neighbouring countries, excluding Muslims.

  • Triggered nationwide protests — the largest since independence.
  • Critics call it unconstitutional and discriminatory.
  • Supreme Court has issued a stay on its implementation.

Farm Laws (Repealed)

2020 – Three contentious agricultural laws that were passed without proper consultation, leading to a year‑long farmers' protest.

  • Withdrawn in 2021 after unprecedented protests.
  • Highlighted the growing disconnect between the government and farmers.
  • Critics called them "anti-farmer" and corporate-friendly.

New Criminal Codes Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita

2023 – Replaced the IPC, CrPC, and Indian Evidence Act with new laws. Critics say the changes are superficial and retain colonial provisions.

  • Sedition replaced with "treason" but remains vague.
  • Increased powers to police, less judicial oversight.
  • Concerns over executive overreach.

The Fake News Epidemic — India's Information Crisis

From WhatsApp forwards to political propaganda, misinformation has become a tool of mass manipulation. Here are some of the most damaging fake news stories that have shaped India's recent history.

2018: The "Love Jihad" Panic

Viral WhatsApp messages claimed Muslim men were forcibly converting Hindu women. The rumour led to violent attacks and lynchings, despite zero evidence.

  • At least 20+ people killed in mob violence.
  • Fact-checkers found 100% of the claims were false.
  • Used as a political weapon in elections.

2020: The "5G Spreads COVID" Hoax

False claims that 5G towers were causing coronavirus infections spread rapidly on social media, leading to vandalism of telecom infrastructure.

  • Over 100 towers damaged across India.
  • Scientific bodies like WHO and ICMR debunked it.
  • Originated from conspiracy groups abroad.

2019: Pulwama Attack — Fake Martyrs

After the Pulwama terror attack, dozens of fake photos and videos of "soldiers" were shared. Some were from old conflicts or even foreign armies.

  • More than 50 verified false images circulated.
  • Used to fuel nationalist sentiment and communal hatred.
  • Fact-checkers flagged them within hours.

2021: The "Vaccine Chips" Conspiracy

Rumours that COVID-19 vaccines contained microchips to track citizens went viral, especially in rural areas, leading to vaccine hesitancy.

  • Vaccination rates dropped by 30% in some districts.
  • Government had to launch massive awareness campaigns.
  • No evidence of any tracking device found.

2022: The "Gujarat Model" Myth

False claims of miraculous economic growth under a certain state government were repeatedly shared, often using manipulated data and old photos.

  • Data was cherry-picked and exaggerated.
  • Opposition parties called it "statistical fiction".
  • Independent economists refuted the claims.

2023: The "White Flag" Propaganda

During a border standoff, a doctored video showing Indian soldiers raising a white flag went viral. It was later proven to be a clip from a military drill.

  • Shared by over 1 million accounts in 24 hours.
  • Used to undermine national morale.
  • Fact-checkers exposed it within 2 days.

⚠️ The Weaponisation of Misinformation

From WhatsApp lynchings to political deepfakes, India has become a global laboratory for misinformation. The lack of a robust fact-checking culture and the speed of social media have allowed false narratives to spread faster than the truth. This is not just a media problem — it's a threat to democracy itself.
